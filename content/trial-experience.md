## Problem
The trial dropped people on the home page and left them to find their own way. Most clicked around for 10 to 20 minutes and left.

A small guided tour existed, buried behind a button, and the people who found it stayed longer and converted more often. We already knew guidance worked. We needed to make it the default.

## Constraints
It was several projects released together: welcome screens, an agent, a home for trial content and a way to buy. Each depended on the others, and the timeline was short.

The company-wide AI push meant the product's tools kept changing while we designed.

## Decision
I designed three surfaces. Welcome screens set expectations, flag the sample data and ask a few questions. The agent, really several agents in a trench coat, uses what we know about the prospect to tailor the trial to their industry and suggests a next step when someone lingers. The trial hub holds everything in a drawer that slides down on any page.

[video trial-hub.mp4 | The trial hub drawer, opened from any page and filtered by topic]

[image versions/trial-hub-page.jpg | The trial hub as its own page, before it moved into a drawer]

Three calls came out of testing. I dropped a chatbot that followed people around, because they had chatbot fatigue. I moved the hub from its own page into a drawer in the trial's black, so people could tell the trial from the product. And I rewrote the agent's suggestions around outcomes, instead of repeating people's pain points back to them.

## Outcome
It didn't ship while I was there. The design was tested and handed off, with release planned for shortly after my role ended, so I can't show conversion numbers.

## Reflection
The brief felt prescribed: put AI in the trial. Each round of testing taught me more about how people feel about AI and what a trial is for. A trial shows how the product looks and feels, and the best thing I could do was make it excellent at that one job.
